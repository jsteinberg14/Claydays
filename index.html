Layering tips

  • Put the stiffer glaze on the bottom and the runnier one on top.
  • Keep the top glaze off the lower third of the piece so it has room to flow.
  • Two to three coats of each glaze is the usual starting point. When dipping, a 2 to 4 second dip is typical.
  • Try a new combo on a test tile or small cup set on a drip tray before glazing a whole batch.
  • Clay color changes the result. Translucent glazes look lighter on white clay and darker on brown or black clay.
  • Cooling matters. Some glazes, like Ancient Copper, change color with a slow cool.

Swatches show approximate glaze colors. Open a combo to see the maker’s fired example and potters’ photos, or add your own photo. Makers fire their examples on white stoneware, so results on your clay will differ. Browse many more in AMACO’s layering database.

Troubleshooting

Tap a problem to see why it happens and how to fix it.

S-cracks in the bottomBowls, mugs

What you see: An S or curved crack in the floor of a pot, often found after bisque.

Why it happens: The floor wasn’t compressed, water sat in the bottom, or the base dried faster or slower than the walls.

How to fix it: Compress the inside floor with a rib or sponge as you throw, sponge out any water, and dry pots slowly, flipping them once the rims firm up.

Cracks while dryingRims, handles, joins

What you see: Cracks at the rim, at handles or where pieces join, before firing.

Why it happens: Thin parts dried faster than thick parts, or two pieces were joined at different dryness.

How to fix it: Dry slowly under loose plastic, wrap rims and handles, join pieces at the same leather-hard stage, and always score and slip.

Handles cracking or popping offMugs, pitchers

What you see: Gaps or cracks where the handle meets the body.

Why it happens: The handle and mug were at different moisture, or the join wasn’t blended in.

How to fix it: Attach handles when both are leather hard, score and slip both surfaces, smooth the join with a finger or tool, and dry under plastic for a day.

Warped rims or platesPlates, wide bowls

What you see: Rims turn oval or plates rock after drying or firing.

Why it happens: Uneven wall thickness, uneven drying, an uneven kiln shelf, or firing past the clay’s cone.

How to fix it: Keep walls even, dry flat pieces slowly and evenly (weighting the rim helps), use a flat shelf, and fire to the cone on the clay bag.

Glaze crawlingAny glaze

What you see: Glaze pulls back into beads or islands, leaving bare clay.

Why it happens: Dust, oil or water on the bisque, glaze put on too thick, or a coat that cracked as it dried.

How to fix it: Wipe bisque with a damp sponge, keep hand lotion off pieces, apply thinner coats and let each coat dry before the next.

Pinholes and blistersAny glaze

What you see: Tiny holes or bubbles in the fired glaze surface.

Why it happens: Gases escaping from the clay or glaze, a very thick application, or a firing that ended too quickly.

How to fix it: Bisque to the recommended cone, stir and apply glaze evenly, avoid overly thick layers, and try a short hold at peak temperature or a slower cool.

CrazingGlazed ware

What you see: A fine network of cracks in the glaze, sometimes appearing days or weeks later.

Why it happens: The glaze shrinks more than the clay as it cools, so it is stretched until it cracks.

How to fix it: Use a glaze and clay that are made to fit, fire to the full recommended cone, or test a different clay body. Crazed food surfaces can trap bacteria, so keep them off the inside of mugs and bowls.

ShiveringGlazed ware

What you see: Flakes of glaze chip off rims and edges.

Why it happens: The opposite of crazing: the glaze is squeezed too hard by the clay.

How to fix it: Switch the glaze or clay. Don’t use shivered pieces for food or drink, because the flakes are sharp.

Glaze ran onto the kiln shelfRunny glazes

What you see: Glaze pooled at the foot or stuck the pot to the shelf.

Why it happens: Too thick a layer, a fluid glaze near the bottom, or firing hotter than the glaze likes.

How to fix it: Keep runny glazes off the bottom third, wipe the foot clean, fire test pieces on a drip tray or cookie, and check the glaze’s cone range.

Cracks after firing (dunting)Fired ware

What you see: Sharp cracks right through a pot after the kiln is opened.

Why it happens: The piece cooled too quickly or had very uneven thickness.

How to fix it: Let the kiln cool fully before opening, keep walls and bases even, and avoid placing pieces near open peepholes during cooling.

BloatingClay body

What you see: Bumps or bubbles in the clay itself after glaze firing.

Why it happens: The clay was fired hotter than it’s made for, or the bisque was too fast to burn everything out.

How to fix it: Fire to the cone on the clay bag and use a slower bisque firing.

Clay won’t center or walls collapseWheel

What you see: Clay wobbles off-center, or walls twist and slump while throwing.

Why it happens: Clay too soft or too much water, hands not braced, or the wheel spinning too fast for the stage.

How to fix it: Brace your elbows, use just enough water, cone the clay up and down to center, slow the wheel as the walls get taller, and let soft clay firm up.

Patchy or streaky glazeBrushed glaze

What you see: Brush marks, light spots or uneven color after firing.

Why it happens: Glaze not stirred, too few coats, or coats applied before the last one dried.

How to fix it: Stir well, brush on 2 to 3 even coats in different directions, and let each coat dry to the touch first.

Convert a temperature

Temperatures are for Orton self-supporting cones heated at 108°F (60°C) per hour over the last 200°F of the firing. Firing slower or faster changes when a cone bends, so use witness cones to check. The dot shows roughly how the kiln glows at that heat.
